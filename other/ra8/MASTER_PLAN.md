# THE ENDLESS VOYAGE — Full Roblox Game Design and Development Plan

Project type: Roblox multiplayer survival, crafting, exploration, and base-building game
Working title: The Endless Voyage (released as **Build the Biggest Raft**)
Core audience: Players who enjoy collecting, building, exploration, progression, and playing with friends
Design target: Fun with 1 player, better with 2, scalable to groups
Business objective: Build a highly replayable Roblox experience with strong retention, organic social growth, and
sustainable monetization.

## 1. Executive Summary

### The concept

You begin stranded in the middle of a vast ocean on a tiny floating platform.

You have almost nothing. Debris drifts past. Mysterious islands appear on the horizon. Something enormous moves
beneath the water.

You collect resources, craft tools, expand your raft, build a home, and eventually transform a few wooden platforms
into an enormous floating fortress capable of traveling across dangerous waters.

Your raft persists between sessions. You can log in for a short session to improve it, or embark on a longer
expedition to explore islands, recover lost technology, find rare creatures, and survive extraordinary ocean events.

Friends can visit your raft, join your voyages, contribute to construction, discover secrets, and share the rewards
of exploration.

The world provides endless reasons to improve your vessel.

### The central promise

Start with nothing. Build something enormous. Explore an ocean full of secrets.

### The five pillars

1. Visible progression: The raft transforms dramatically over time.
2. Discovery: The ocean constantly offers new things to find.
3. Freedom: Players can make progress alone without waiting for friends.
4. Social ownership: The raft becomes something worth showing off.
5. Adventure: Expeditions create memorable risks, rewards, and stories.

### The most important design decision

The raft must be more than a building area.

It should affect nearly every aspect of gameplay:

* How far you can travel
* What resources you can collect
* Which islands you can reach
* How you survive storms
* Which ocean creatures you can fight
* How much cargo you can carry
* Which technologies you can research
* What other players see when they visit

A larger raft should unlock new possibilities, not merely provide more floor space.

## 2. Target Audience and Player Motivations

The game should appeal to several overlapping groups.

**Builders.** They want to turn a tiny raft into an impressive creation. They enjoy arranging rooms, adding decks,
collecting furniture, and showing off their builds.

**Collectors.** They want rare materials, fish, artifacts, decorations, equipment, creature discoveries, and limited
cosmetics.

**Explorers.** They want to visit strange islands, enter abandoned structures, uncover secrets, and find things other
players have not discovered.

**Progression-focused players.** They want stronger tools, better equipment, new ship systems, more efficient
production, and access to increasingly difficult regions.

**Social players.** They want to invite friends, visit impressive rafts, trade discoveries, cooperate on voyages,
and compare accomplishments.

**Competitive players.** They want to complete challenges faster, discover rare items first, defeat difficult
encounters, and rank highly in selected leaderboards.

The experience should not force players into a single motivation. A builder should be able to spend a session
decorating, while an explorer can spend the same session searching for a rare island.

## 3. The Core Gameplay Loop

The primary loop is:

Collect → Build → Upgrade → Sail → Explore → Discover → Return → Improve → Repeat

**Step 1: Collect.** Use hooks, nets, salvage equipment, fishing tools, and later automated collection systems to
recover materials from the ocean.

**Step 2: Build.** Expand the raft, add storage, construct crafting stations, improve defenses, and personalize the
vessel.

**Step 3: Upgrade.** Unlock better tools, engines, navigation, cargo systems, research, and equipment.

**Step 4: Sail.** Choose a destination or voyage. Travel consumes time and exposes the raft to different environments
and threats.

**Step 5: Explore.** Visit islands, search wrecks, collect resources, solve environmental puzzles, and encounter
unusual creatures.

**Step 6: Discover.** Find new crafting recipes, rare materials, hidden blueprints, creature records, artifacts, and
cosmetic rewards.

**Step 7: Return.** Bring home the valuable discoveries, unload cargo, repair damage, and prepare for the next
journey.

**Step 8: Improve.** Use expedition rewards to unlock new raft capabilities and access more dangerous regions.

This loop should remain understandable throughout the entire game. New systems should deepen the loop rather than
replace it with disconnected minigames.

## 4. The First Five Minutes

The opening five minutes are the most important part of the experience.

A new player should not need a friend, a wiki, a tutorial video, or a complicated menu to begin enjoying the game.

**0–15 seconds: Immediate situation.** The player appears on a tiny raft in a beautiful, open ocean. They see: a
small amount of starting equipment, floating debris nearby, a simple collection tool, a clear direction for their
first action. The first objective is short and concrete: "Collect 5 pieces of debris." Movement and camera controls
should already feel responsive. Avoid an opening cutscene or a long explanation of the world.

**15–45 seconds: First reward.** The player collects floating debris. Every pickup should have satisfying feedback:
a clear sound, a brief visual effect, an updated resource count, a visible addition to their inventory. The player
should quickly feel that they are accomplishing something.

**45–90 seconds: First construction.** The player uses the collected resources to add a new floor tile to the raft.
This is the first important emotional moment. The raft is visibly larger because of something the player did.
Immediately introduce one or two simple construction options, not a complete building encyclopedia.

**1.5–2.5 minutes: First useful upgrade.** The player builds or unlocks a basic crafting station, storage container,
or improved collection tool. They learn that resources can become useful equipment, not just additional floor tiles.

**2.5–3.5 minutes: First discovery.** A small landmark appears nearby, or a floating supply crate contains a useful
blueprint. The player is introduced to the idea that the ocean contains more than debris.

**3.5–5 minutes: First meaningful decision.** Present a choice: continue collecting safely; follow a visible island
or wreck; investigate an unusual object in the water. The player should understand that exploration can produce
better rewards but may require preparation.

By minute five, they should have: collected resources, expanded the raft, used a crafting or building system,
discovered a new goal, understood that the game contains a much larger world.

**First-session rules**

* No mandatory group formation.
* No forced spending prompt.
* No complicated skill tree.
* No long survival-meter tutorial.
* No empty opening landscape without meaningful objects.
* No punishment for not understanding an unexplained mechanic.
* Always show one useful next action.
* Give players something permanent to be proud of as early as practical.

The opening should demonstrate the fantasy before explaining its systems.

## 5. The Raft: The Center of the Entire Game

The raft is the most important system in the project. Its progression must be visible, mechanically meaningful, and
emotionally rewarding.

### 5.1 Starting raft

The player begins with a small platform, basic collection equipment, and a simple storage area. The first few
upgrades should happen quickly. The player needs to see the raft change during their first session.

### 5.2 Structural expansion

Players can build: floor tiles, railings, stairs, ladders, support structures, multiple decks, rooms, roofs,
windows, lighting, decorative features. Structural building should feel satisfying without becoming a demanding
architectural simulation.

**Building controls:** mobile-friendly placement controls, clear tile snapping, rotation and repositioning,
placement previews, obvious blocked-placement feedback, simple demolition or relocation rules, reliable saving of
completed builds. Avoid requiring precision mouse placement for routine building tasks.

### 5.3 Functional rooms

Rooms should serve gameplay purposes: a workshop for crafting, an engine room for propulsion, a navigation room for
route planning, a storage room for cargo management, a research room for blueprints, a greenhouse for growing
supplies, an aquarium for displaying discoveries, a trophy room for rare expedition finds, crew quarters for optional
helper characters. These rooms should unlock gradually. The player should not need to understand every room during
the opening session.

### 5.4 Raft progression tiers

Use tiers as a broad guide, not rigid limits on creativity.

* **Tier 1: Driftwood Platform.** A small raft with basic tools and minimal storage. Focus: collecting,
  constructing, and learning.
* **Tier 2: Survival Raft.** A recognizable floating home with storage, a workshop, and basic defenses. Focus:
  sustainable gathering and short trips.
* **Tier 3: Expedition Vessel.** A larger raft with improved propulsion, navigation, expanded cargo, and dedicated
  functional rooms. Focus: island exploration and better equipment.
* **Tier 4: Ocean Explorer.** A multi-deck vessel with advanced technology, specialized stations, and strong
  defenses. Focus: dangerous regions and rare resources.
* **Tier 5: Floating Fortress.** A visually impressive, heavily customized vessel with substantial capabilities.
  Focus: endgame expeditions, major encounters, and prestige.
* **Tier 6: Legendary Vessel.** An aspirational late-game tier with exceptional customization and access to endgame
  challenges. Focus: mastery, rare collections, difficult voyages, and social status.

These are progression milestones, not arbitrary sizes. Each tier must introduce at least one meaningful new
capability.

### 5.5 Avoiding an empty giant raft

A giant platform with nothing to do is not good progression. Larger vessels should unlock: new production options,
specialized workstations, better cargo organization, new expedition tools, more effective defenses, new traversal
options, additional customization, access to advanced regions. The player should feel that the raft is becoming more
capable, not simply more expensive.

### 5.6 Personal ownership

Each player should have a persistent home raft that belongs to their profile. This protects players from losing
their entire creation because a friend leaves or a server closes. The game can create shared expeditions around one
designated host raft or a temporary expedition vessel, but the ownership rules must be explicit.

Recommended initial rule:

* Every player owns a persistent raft.
* Friends can visit it with permission.
* An expedition host can bring their raft into a session.
* Guests can contribute to expedition objectives.
* Guests cannot permanently alter another player's build without permission.
* Shared expedition rewards follow clearly communicated rules.

The first version should avoid complex simultaneous ownership of one persistent raft by several independent player
accounts.

## 6. Persistent Progression and Expedition Progression

The game should combine permanent development with meaningful short-term adventures.

**Permanent progression.** The player retains: their saved raft, permanent blueprints and research, their discovered
locations and creature records, selected equipment unlocks, achievements, cosmetics, collection records, account
progression.

**Expedition progression.** A voyage can involve: temporary cargo, mission objectives, voyage-specific supplies,
temporary equipment, encounter damage, optional bonus objectives, expedition rewards awaiting extraction or return.

The key question is what happens if a player fails.

**Recommended rule.** Ordinary exploration should not erase hours of permanent progress. If the raft is damaged or an
expedition fails: the saved raft remains; permanent discoveries remain; unsecured expedition loot may be lost; cargo
loss should be limited and clearly communicated; repairs should be achievable without an excessive grind; failure
should create a story and a reason to prepare better, not make players quit.

High-risk voyages can introduce greater potential losses later, but they should be opt-in and understandable.

## 7. Ocean Collection and Resource Gathering

Collecting is the first half of the progression loop. It must be satisfying at the beginning and gradually become
more strategic.

### 7.1 Floating debris

Common ocean pickups: wood, plastic or reusable scrap, rope, fabric, metal fragments, supply containers, occasional
unusual objects. The visual design should make common pickups easy to recognize from a distance.

### 7.2 Collection tools

Start with one simple collection tool, then introduce alternatives. Potential progression: basic hook, improved
hook, wider-reach collection tool, automatic surface net, advanced salvage collector, specialized collectors for rare
materials. Tools should improve collection in different ways, not only increase a numerical stat.

### 7.3 Nets and automated collection

Nets can collect nearby debris while the player performs other activities. Later systems might include: improved
collection radius, multiple collection lanes, specialized salvage nets, cargo sorting, rare-object detection,
automated collection modules. Automation should reduce repetitive tasks while leaving players meaningful decisions.
Do not automate the entire early game. Collecting needs to teach the player how the world works.

### 7.4 Resource tiers

Common resources (basic construction and everyday crafting), processed resources (created from common materials
through workshops or machines), rare resources (found in specific locations, wrecks, or dangerous regions), advanced
materials (higher-tier engines, equipment, and technology), unique artifacts (research, collection completion,
special blueprints, or prestige). Rare materials should have several possible uses so that discoveries feel valuable
even after a player has built one specific item.

### 7.5 Resource economy

Avoid making every upgrade require huge quantities of common materials. A healthy economy has: frequent small
rewards, occasional substantial discoveries, clear upgrade targets, multiple ways to earn useful resources,
increasingly efficient gathering tools, enough material sinks to prevent infinite accumulation. Players should usually
be working toward a goal rather than endlessly grinding without knowing why.

## 8. Crafting and Research

Crafting turns resources into capability. Research gives exploration a long-term purpose.

**Crafting stations.** Basic Workbench (tools, simple weapons, structural components, and repairs). Refining Station
(processed materials and advanced construction ingredients). Engineering Bench (engines, collection systems, power
components, and specialized equipment). Research Station (blueprint discovery, technology unlocks, and expedition
analysis). Cooking or Supply Station (food, healing items, and optional temporary buffs). Decorative Workshop
(furniture, colors, cosmetic structures, and trophies).

**Recipe design.** Each recipe should have: a clear purpose, a readable cost, a visible result, a meaningful reason
to build it, a reasonable progression position. Avoid hundreds of recipes at launch. A smaller set of useful choices
is better than an enormous catalog full of redundant items.

**Blueprint discovery.** Blueprints can be found through: island exploration, wreck salvage, research milestones,
boss or creature encounters, hidden environmental puzzles, collection completion, optional challenge rewards.
Blueprints should persist once unlocked. A rare blueprint can be exciting without requiring the player to repeat the
same task hundreds of times.

## 9. Exploration: Islands, Wrecks, and Secrets

Exploration is the system that prevents the game from becoming only a building simulator.

### 9.1 Island design

Each island should have a readable identity, a reason to visit, and at least one discovery. Examples: a tropical
island with wood, plants, and basic supplies; a rocky island with metal deposits; a fog-covered island with
abandoned structures; a small volcanic island with heat-resistant materials; a frozen island with rare minerals; an
overgrown ruin containing old technology. The first release should focus on a small number of polished island
templates with meaningful variations.

### 9.2 Island activities

Players can: gather resources, search containers, discover landmarks, solve simple environmental puzzles, find hidden
passages, photograph or catalog unusual wildlife, recover blueprints, complete optional challenges, face local
creatures, return to their raft with discoveries. Not every island needs combat. Quiet discovery is an important part
of the pacing.

### 9.3 Shipwrecks

Wrecks are high-value exploration destinations. They may contain: rare salvage, damaged technology, historical logs,
blueprint fragments, decorative relics, dangerous creatures, hidden rooms, optional challenge objectives. Wrecks
should have different risk levels, from small surface wrecks to difficult late-game locations.

### 9.4 Environmental storytelling

The ocean should feel like a world with a past. Possible clues: abandoned camps, strange structures, damaged
vessels, messages in containers, unusual geological formations, strange lights below the surface, signs of a much
larger creature. The story can remain mysterious. Players should be able to enjoy the game without reading lengthy
lore.

### 9.5 Discovery journal

Maintain a permanent record of: islands visited, wrecks explored, resources found, creatures encountered, artifacts
recovered, rare events witnessed, secrets discovered. Completionists should always have another discovery to pursue.

## 10. Navigation and Travel

Travel must feel like an activity, not simply a loading screen.

**Early travel.** The player starts with simple propulsion, such as paddling or a small sail. Travel is slow enough
to create anticipation but not so slow that the player feels trapped.

**Midgame travel.** Unlock: improved sails, steering upgrades, basic engines, navigation instruments, better route
planning, more efficient fuel or power systems.

**Late-game travel.** Unlock advanced propulsion, specialized navigation, and access to difficult regions. The late
game should feel like the player has become a capable ocean explorer.

**Navigation interface.** Provide: a clear compass, known landmarks, destination markers when appropriate, a map that
gradually fills in, discovered route records, optional challenge routes, environmental warnings. Avoid revealing
every secret automatically. Players should still be able to discover things.

**Travel pacing.** Use a combination of: short trips for common resources, medium expeditions for blueprints and
island exploration, longer voyages for rare materials and major objectives. A player with only ten minutes should
still be able to complete a useful activity.
